Acemoglu (2024) and Aghion & Bunel (2024) use the same formula to forecast Total Factor Productivity (TFP) impact from AI.

A comparison of their papers shows the importance of alternative assumptions.

We created this tool to let you be part of the discussion. Change the inputs according to what you think is most accurate and determine TFP growth within the tasked-based model from Acemoglu (2024). As AI research advances, estimates may change. Therefore, this tool is built to be updated, and we invite you to share new studies with us.

We also present other models and estimates of TFP, Labor Productivity, and GDP Growth, demonstrating the vast array of opinions within this subject.

How each economist gets there

Acemoglu

20%

From Eloundou et al. (2024) and combined into occupations using wage bills from BLS - resulting in bill weighted share of exposed occupations. Interpreted as GDP share of tasks exposed to AI.

Aghion & Bunel

60%

From Pizzinelli et al. (2023), who measure AI exposure by abilities. Abilities are weighted by prevalence and importance and aggregated to occupations, resulting in AI occupational exposure (AIOE) by occupation and employment shares by country. The 60% is US-specific and includes both high and low complementarity employment.

Key differenceAcemoglu uses task exposure while Aghion & Bunel use ability exposure. Aghion & Bunel flag occupations above the AIOE median as exposed to AI; Acemoglu uses each occupation's share of exposed tasks, then weights by wage bill to get a GDP share. Aghion & Bunel apply no weighting, assuming the GDP share of tasks exposed to AI is similar in magnitude to the share of tasks exposed to AI (despite using abilities).

Other Considerations

Acemoglu's variants

  • 0.53% — hard-to-learn tasks. 72.6% of exposed tasks by wage bill are easy; cost savings on the rest assumed at 7% rather than 27%. The estimate he considers most reasonable.
  • 0.66% — baseline. 4.6% of GDP impacted × 14.4% overall cost savings. He calls it an upper bound.
  • 0.89% — including Peng et al. Labor cost savings rise from 27% to 36%.
  • 0.90% — rapid compute cost decline. Svanberg et al.'s (2024) 10%-a-year scenario lifts feasibly automated tasks to 30%.

Aghion & Bunel's intervals

  • 18.5%–68% — share of GDP exposed.
  • 23%–80% — share profitable to automate.
  • 27%–40% — average cost savings.
  • 57% — labor share, fixed, taken from Acemoglu.
